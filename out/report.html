shape, beta
The headline. The fitted shape parameter is beta = 0.918, with a 95 % interval of 0.823 to 1.023 that contains 1. Over a race distance an F1 car has an essentially constant hazard: it is no more likely to break in the last quarter of a race than in the first. A likelihood ratio against the exponential gives p = 0.117, so the simpler model cannot be rejected. Counting the finishers as failures instead — the commonest way to mishandle censored data — returns beta = 3.53, a confident wear-out signature that is not there.

Why 2014 to 2021

The later boundary is the feed, not a choice. From 2022 the data stops recording why a car retired, and a season that reports only "Retired" cannot separate an engine failure from a crash.

The earlier boundary is an engineering choice: 2014 opened the V6 turbo hybrid regulations, and a reliability model pooled across a regulation change is fitting two different machines at once.

A crash is not a reliability event. It is a competing risk: something else that can end the car's race first and so prevent the failure under study from ever being observed.

eta is an extrapolation. The characteristic life comes out at 11.4 race distances, but no car in the data runs past one. The fit sees only the first 9 % of that life, so eta and anything derived from it — the mean life above all — rest on the Weibull shape holding far beyond where it was observed. The B10 life of 0.983 sits at the edge of the data and is on much firmer ground.

The probability plot is a check the fit does not grade itself on: the transformed Kaplan-Meier points give a slope of 0.825 against the maximum likelihood 0.918, with R-squared 0.9620.

Neither wrong method is a rough approximation of the right one. Dropping the survivors throws away every long life and collapses the scale from 11.41 to 0.52; counting them as failures manufactures a population that nearly all dies at the same distance, which is what beta = 3.53 means.

Competing risks

Treating crashes as censoring assumes a car that crashes was just as likely to have failed later as one that did not — and then answers the question "what fraction would fail if crashes were abolished". The Aalen-Johansen estimator answers the question actually asked.

quantityvalue
P(technical failure by the flag), Aalen-Johansen 0.0968

The correction is real but modest here, and saying so is part of the result: the bias grows with the competing event rate, and at a 7 % crash rate there is not much of it to grow from.
